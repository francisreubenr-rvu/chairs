Design Thinking

A committee agenda arrives as a topic, not a problem. This module is how you turn one into the other, and then into a solution you can defend.

What is Design Thinking DT p.2

An iterative process, and a solution-based approach

  • It is iterative. We seek to understand the user, challenge assumptions, and redefine problems in an attempt to identify alternative strategies and solutions.Solutions that might not be instantly apparent with our initial level of understanding.
  • It is solution-based. Design Thinking provides a solution-based approach to solving problems.
  • It is both a mindset and a method. It is a way of thinking and working, as well as a collection of hands-on methods.
Read the definition again for the phrase "challenge assumptions". That is the same move as climbing down the Ladder of Inference in Module 2. Design Thinking is the structured version of it.
Four claims in the definition, each doing work
IterativeYou will revisit phases, not pass through them once
User-centredUnderstanding comes before solving
Assumption-challengingIncluding your own portfolio's
Solution-basedThe output is an answer, not a diagnosis
Where the five-phase model comes from The Empathise–Define–Ideate–Prototype–Test formulation is the model taught by the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford, commonly called the d.school. It is a teaching frame, not a law: other organisations describe the same work in three phases or six.
Solution-based, not problem-based DT p.2 Added

Why the distinction matters in committee

Both approaches are legitimate. They produce very different speeches.

Problem-based
  • Starts by fully characterising the obstacle
  • Asks: what exactly is wrong, and why
  • Produces analysis
  • In committee: an accurate diagnosis of the agenda that proposes nothing. The chair has heard it four times already.
Solution-based
  • Starts from a desired outcome and works back
  • Asks: what would a working answer look like
  • Produces options to test
  • In committee: a Working Paper. The Rules of Procedure require a Solution on the Agenda in your very first speech.
The rules are already solution-based. Speakers List speech: POV plus Solution. Moderated Caucus speech: POV plus Solution on the Sub-Topic. Working Paper: solution to the sub-topic. Resolution: solution to the main agenda. Every document in the committee is an answer, not a description.
Phases of Design Thinking DT p.3

Five phases

The phases are not a queue. The process is iterative: testing sends you back to define, and defining sends you back to empathise. Expect to move backwards at least once.
What each phase produces
EmpathiseObservations about real people
DefineOne problem statement
IdeateOptions, then a selection
PrototypeSomething concrete to react to
TestA named failed assumption
Phase 1 · Empathise DT p.3 Added

Understand the user, not your idea of the user

Empathise means setting aside your assumptions to gain insight into the people affected. In a committee context, your user is whoever the agenda actually lands on.

Methods
  • ObserveWhat people do, which often differs from what they say they do
  • EngageAsk open questions. "Tell me about the last time…" beats "Do you find X difficult?"
  • ImmerseExperience the constraint yourself where you safely can
  • Read the recordTestimony, reports and first-hand accounts stand in for access you do not have
In committee
  • Name the affected population precisely"Rural households without piped supply" not "people in developing countries"
  • Separate them from your portfolioYour portfolio's interest and your user's need are not the same thing. Know where they diverge before an opponent finds it.
  • Source the needAn empathy claim with no source is challengeable under Point of Information
Phase 2 · Define DT p.3 Added

Turn observations into one stated problem

Define converts what you gathered into a problem statement you can actually solve. The source asks for three things: your users' needs, their problem, and your insights.

Point-of-view statement
[User] needs a way to [need] because [insight].
  • The need is a verb, not a solution"needs a way to store water safely", not "needs a water tank"
  • The insight is the surprising partIf the "because" clause is obvious, you have not defined anything yet
How Might We
  • Reframe the point of view as an opening question"How might we…" is deliberately open: it presumes a solution exists without naming it
  • Too broad gives no traction"How might we end poverty?"
  • Too narrow smuggles the answer in"How might we build cheaper tanks?"
  • Right-sized constrains without decidingThe eight challenges on the next slide are written in exactly this form
Phase 3 · Ideate DT p.3 Added

Challenge assumptions and generate options

The source is explicit that ideation works by challenging assumptions. Generating volume is the mechanism; challenging assumptions is the purpose.

Diverge first
  • Separate generating from judgingEvaluating during generation kills the unusual options first, and those are the ones you came for
  • Go for quantity before qualityThe obvious ideas surface early; the useful ones arrive after you exhaust them
  • Build on others' ideas"Yes, and" rather than "yes, but"
  • Invert the problemAsk how you would guarantee failure, then reverse each answer
Then converge
  • ClusterGroup ideas by the mechanism they rely on, not by theme
  • Select against criteria you set beforeOtherwise you will pick the idea you liked at the start
  • Keep one wild cardThe bold option is what makes a caucus remember your bloc
Unmoderated Caucus is your ideation slot. It runs a maximum of 20 minutes and its stated uses include lobbying, formation of blocks and drafting documents. Electronic equipment is permitted during it.
Phase 4 · Prototype DT p.3 Added

Start creating solutions, cheaply

A prototype exists to be wrong in front of you rather than in front of the committee. Its only job is to make the idea concrete enough to react to.

FidelityFormAnswers
SketchA drawing or diagram on one pageDoes the idea hold together at all?
StoryboardThe user's journey, frame by frameWhere does it break for the person using it?
Paper or physical mock-upA non-working stand-in you can hand to someoneIs the interaction obvious without explanation?
Role-playAct the service out with peopleDoes the human part work?
Working PaperThe written solution to the sub-topic, with no fixed formatWill the committee support it as drafted?
The Working Paper is a prototype in the procedural sense. It has no format, requires no signatories, and passes by Simple Majority. If passed, it should be incorporated in the Resolution. It is designed to be revised.
Fidelity is a budget decision Build the cheapest thing that can still be wrong in a useful way. A sketch answers "does this hold together"; only a role-play answers "does the human part work". Spending a week on a polished prototype before you know which question you are asking is the commonest waste in this phase.
Phase 5 · Test DT p.3 Added

Test solutions, then go back a phase

Testing is not approval-seeking. You are looking for the fastest possible disproof of your own solution.

What to look for
  • Where they hesitate: confusion beats politeness as a signal
  • What they work around: the workaround is the real requirement
  • What they ignore: the parts you were proudest of, usually
  • Which assumption failed: name it explicitly before you iterate
Where it sends you
  • Mechanism failed → back to Ideate
  • Solved the wrong problem → back to Define
  • Misread who is affected → back to Empathise
  • Held up → raise it as a Working Paper
Trainer practice The committee tests your solution for you, for free, through Challenge, Question Answer Session and One for One Against. Treat the first hostile question as data, not as an attack.
Design Thinking with SDGs DT p.4

Group challenges · 1 to 4

Each challenge is stated as a How Might We question. That is deliberate.

Group 1 · SDG 1: No Poverty
  • How might we create innovative ways to provide affordable housing for underprivileged families?
Group 2 · SDG 3: Good Health and Well-Being
  • How might we design a solution to promote mental health awareness among teenagers?
Group 3 · SDG 5: Gender Equality
  • How might we ensure equal access to education and technology for girls in rural areas?
Group 4 · SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities
  • How might we design eco-friendly transportation options to reduce traffic congestion in urban areas?
Read the wording Every challenge opens with "How might we". That is a Define-phase device: it asserts a solution exists without naming it, so the group cannot skip straight to their favourite answer. Resist rewriting the challenge into a solution before you have run Empathise.
Design Thinking with SDGs DT p.4

Group challenges · 5 to 8

Group 5 · SDG 14: Life Below Water
  • How might we reduce plastic waste in oceans through community-driven initiatives?
Group 6 · SDG 4: Quality Education
  • How might we make learning more fun and accessible for children who do not have internet or digital devices?
Group 7 · SDG 6: Clean Water and Sanitation
  • How might we help rural communities get clean drinking water using low-cost and sustainable solutions?
Group 8 · SDG 13: Climate Action
  • How might we encourage students and families to take small actions every day to fight climate change?
Each of these hides a user you have not met Before ideating: who exactly is the plastic-waste initiative asking to change behaviour, and what does their week look like? Which children lack devices, and what do they currently learn with? Named users produce specific solutions; unnamed users produce slogans.
Design Thinking with SDG 12 DT p.5 Added mapping

Six contents to cover in the discussion

The six required contents map onto the five phases. Use the mapping to check you have not skipped a phase.

ContentPhase it evidencesThe test
a. IntroductionEmpathise + DefineWho is affected, and what is the stated problem?
b. Brainstormed IdeasIdeate: divergeDid you generate beyond the first obvious answer?
c. Selected IdeasIdeate: convergeOn what criteria, decided when?
d. Plan of ActionPrototypeWho does what, and what would it cost?
e. Benefits & ImpactTestImpact on whom, measured how?
f. Challenges & SolutionTest: iterateWhat failed, and what did you change?
Section f is where marks are won. A presentation with no challenges listed has either not tested the idea or is hiding the result. Both read the same way to an examiner.
