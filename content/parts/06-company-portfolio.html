The whole module in one question md · intro

What would this company actually say in this room, and can you prove it from a primary source?

Every resource in this module is chosen to answer that one question. The spine is unchanged from the country track: Portfolio → Committee → Agenda → Policy Stance → Solution.

The failure mode is specific and checkable. A country delegate who invents a policy may not be caught. A company delegate who invents a stance can be refuted in seconds by any delegate with the 10-K open. Corporate positions are filed, dated and public.
Four questions the module answers in order
Who is this companyOwnership, business lines, scale (§3.1, §3.2)
What has it been held toAntitrust, litigation, lobbying (§3.3)
Who does it stand besideCompetitors and the association line (§3.4)
Who argues against itNGO and watchdog reports (§3.5)
The asymmetry to keep in mind A country's position can be genuinely ambiguous. A public company's position is filed, dated and searchable. That cuts both ways: it is harder to bluff, and far easier to prove.

The official line, and the adversarial view

§3.4 Industry & competitive context
  • Industry association siteThe "official line" companies rarely deviate from publicly

§3.5 Reputation, ESG & stakeholder signals
  • GlassdoorEmployee sentiment and internal culture signals
  • MSCI ESG Ratings / SustainalyticsThird-party scoring. Cross-check against the company's own ESG report
  • NGO / watchdog reportsAmnesty Business & Human Rights, HRW corporate accountability, Greenpeace campaigns. The adversarial view, essential for anticipating hostile questions.
  • Trustpilot / review aggregatorsConsumer-facing reputation signal
Read the adversarial source last, and never skip it NGO and watchdog reports are written to be damaging, which is exactly their value: they are a preview of the hardest question you will face. Reading one before committee turns an ambush into a prepared answer.

Zero-to-hero track · stages 0–2 md §5

Orient, profile, map the sector

Stage 0
~30 min
Orientation: reframe from "citizen-representative" to corporate self-interestDiscuss who this company serves and what it wants out of this committee. Deliverable: a one-paragraph statement of the company's core interest in the agenda.
Stage 1
2–3 hrs
Company Profile: build the factual baseFounding, HQ, ownership, leadership, business lines, scale, revenue. Pull from §3.1 and §3.2. Deliverable: a one-page Company Factsheet.
Stage 2
2–3 hrs
Competitive Landscape: map the sectorIdentify the top 2–3 competitors, the industry association, and market position. Pull from §3.3 and §3.4. Deliverable: a Competitive Landscape table (competitor, position, differentiator).
Stage 0 is not optional warm-up. Delegates who skip it argue like a country for the first two sessions, defending the public interest instead of the company's interest, and have to unlearn it.
Stage 0 is 30 minutes and saves hours A delegate who has not made the mindset shift argues for the public interest, because that is the instinct a country portfolio trains. A company may well arrive at a public-interest position, but it gets there through market position, regulatory freedom and shareholder value, and it must be argued that way.
Zero-to-hero track · stages 3–5 md §5

Stance, speech, simulation

Stage 3
3–4 hrs
Policy Stance (PESTEL): the core deliverable, equivalent to the country track's PEST sectionRun PESTEL against regulators in the agenda, competitors, and the industry association line; pull the adversarial NGO view for stress-testing. Deliverable: a Policy Stance Matrix (P-E-S-T-E-L rows × comparator columns).
Stage 4
1.5–2 hrs
Position & Speech Draft: turn research into the 90-second Speakers List speechDraft POV on agenda plus proposed solution; verify every claim against a primary source from stages 1–3. Deliverable: a source-tagged speech draft.
Stage 5
ongoing
Simulation & Crisis Readiness: battle-test the positionMock caucusing, Challenge rounds, Q&A defending the stance live; practise citing the 10-K, court filings and testimony from memory. 2–3 mock sessions before the conference.
Total dedicated prep: roughly 10–13 hours, front-loaded before conference week, with Stage 5 repeated as mock-session slots allow.

The prep routine · same for every student Portfolio Prep p.1

Eight steps, one evening, any portfolio

A company delegate is not there to be good or bad. They are there to protect their organisation's interests while proving it can be part of the solution.

01
Know your portfolio in five linesWhat it does, where it operates, its size, its biggest strength, its biggest controversy.
02
Know the agendaThree facts and one statistic on the committee's topic.
03
Find the overlapHow the portfolio is affected by the agenda, and what it can contribute: money, technology, data, training, reach.
04
Write a 60–90 second opening speechHook, who we are, our stand, one concrete proposal.
05
Prepare two or three solutionsFor each: who pays, who runs it, and how success is measured.
06
Map allies and criticsWho naturally agrees, who will attack, and one compromise to offer each.
07
Prepare for the hardest question about your portfolioHave a calm two-line answer ready before anyone asks it.
08
Stay in character"Sony believes…", never "I think…".
Every speech answers three questions What does my organisation want, what can it offer, and who do I need on my side? Write those three answers down before you write a single line of the speech.
Worked portfolio · culture and skills Portfolio Prep p.1

Two companies, two committees, one routine

The same eight steps, run on a company that sells experience and a company that sells a workforce pipeline.

Sony Interactive Entertainment · Future Tourism Forum
  • PurposeThe voice of games and digital entertainment as a driver of travel and culture
  • Games create travel demandGhost of Tsushima drew attention to Tsushima island in Japan
  • Virtual and VR tourismLets people preview destinations and makes heritage sites accessible to people who cannot travel
  • Partnerships with tourism boardsShowcase real places in games; protect fragile sites by offering digital alternatives
  • AlliesTourism boards, culture and heritage bodies, tech firms
Tough question: "Doesn't virtual tourism hurt real local economies?" Answer: it is a trailer, not a replacement; it brings visitors in and spreads them to less crowded places.
Boeing · Education Committee
  • PurposeAn industry employer that needs a skilled future workforce, so it has a stake in what schools teach
  • STEM and engineering skillsAerospace and engineering, especially for girls and underrepresented students
  • Industry-school partnershipsInternships, apprenticeships, mentoring, lab funding
  • The demand is realAviation needs huge numbers of pilots, engineers and technicians in the coming decades
  • AlliesTech companies, universities, labour and skills bodies
Tough question: "Why trust Boeing after the 737 MAX safety failures?" Answer: that is exactly why it now stresses safety culture, ethics and quality in training. Admit it, then pivot.
Worked portfolio · labour and data Portfolio Prep p.2

A bridge-builder and a data company

One portfolio is the natural chair of the room. The other has to earn its seat by offering capability rather than territory.

International Labour Organization · Education & HR
  • PurposeThe UN agency for work, founded 1919. Tripartite: governments, employers and workers decide together. Its mission is decent work.
  • Education must lead to decent jobsSkills mismatch, vocational training, lifelong learning and reskilling for AI
  • Fair workFair hiring, no discrimination, safe workplaces, ending child labour so children stay in school
  • Bring the three parties to one tableThe ILO is the natural bridge-builder in the room
  • AlliesAlmost everyone; it should draft resolutions and broker compromises
Tough question: "ILO standards are only on paper, how do you enforce them?" Answer: ratified conventions, country reporting and technical support, plus pressure on companies through their supply chains.
Palantir · World Council on Future Health and Food
  • PurposeA data-analytics company. It does not grow food or treat patients; it helps governments and organisations see and decide with data.
  • Health data platformsIt runs NHS England's Federated Data Platform, for hospital waiting lists, vaccine rollout and disease tracking
  • Food supply chainsIt has partnered with the UN World Food Programme to reduce waste and get food to people faster
  • Early warningFamine, outbreaks and shortages, through better data
  • AlliesGovernments, WHO and WFP-type bodies, logistics firms
Tough question: "What about privacy and your work with militaries and surveillance?" Answer: data stays owned and controlled by the client, with access controls and audit trails; propose strict data-protection rules in the resolution.
How they win the committee Portfolio Prep p.2

Five moves, all of them before the vote

None of these is a trick. Each one converts preparation into something the committee can see and cite.

  1. Speak early The first few speeches set the debate. Being third is worth more than being perfect on the fifth attempt.
  2. Offer something concrete "Sony will fund…", "Palantir will provide…" beats general talk. A named contribution is a clause waiting to be written.
  3. Lobby in breaks Find two or three delegates and co-write clauses. The resolution is built outside the speakers list as much as inside it.
  4. Handle attacks calmly Acknowledge the point, then turn to a solution. The delegate who concedes one fact and pivots keeps the room.
  5. Get named on the resolution As a sponsor or signatory. Influence that is not on the paper does not count at voting time.
The pattern across all four portfolios Sony offers reach, Boeing offers training, the ILO offers process, Palantir offers data. Each one wins by turning its comparative advantage into a sentence another delegate can sign.
How a company delegate researches Research Sources p.1

Primary first, and cite it in one line

Primary sources are the company's own voice. Secondary sources are the rules and global frameworks that constrain it. Cite primary before secondary, and never cite an aggregator in place of either.

Primary · cite these first
  • Annual report or 10-K / 20-FFacts, risks and strategy, on the Investor Relations page or SEC EDGAR (sec.gov/edgar/search)
  • Sustainability / ESG / Integrated ReportOfficial positions on people, data, ethics and the environment
  • Newsroom / press releasesRecent moves, product launches and CEO statements
  • Published policiesAI ethics, data privacy, human rights and code of conduct
Citation format: Company, Title of Report, Year, page or section, URL.
Example: SAP SE, Integrated Report 2025, "Sustainability" section, sap.com/integrated-reports/2025.
Secondary · the rules business answers to
  • UN Global Compactunglobalcompact.org. Check whether the company is a participant
  • UN Guiding Principles on Business & Human Rightsohchr.org
  • OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprisesmneguidelines.oecd.org
  • Business & Human Rights Resource Centrebusiness-humanrights.org. Allegations and company responses
  • World Economic Forum reportsweforum.org/publications
  • Reuters · Financial Times · Bloomberg · The Economist · UN NewsNews, cited as secondary
  • PubMed · Google ScholarScience
Pre-committee checklist md §7

Eight boxes, all ticked before you walk in

  • Company Factsheet complete (§3.1, §3.2)
  • Top 2–3 competitors and industry association position identified (§3.3, §3.4)
  • PESTEL Policy Stance Matrix filled, cross-checked against an adversarial source (§3.5)
  • Every claim in the speech draft is source-tagged, no claim without a citation
  • Adversarial / NGO view reviewed so hostile questions are not a surprise
  • At least one primary-source figure, financial or legal, memorised for live citation
  • Google News Alert live on the company name through conference week
  • Mock Challenge round completed at least once before the real committee
Box 6 is the one delegates skip. Reading a figure and being able to state it live under a Point of Information are different skills. Memorise one number and its source.
If you only have one evening Do boxes 1, 4 and 6. A Company Factsheet, a speech where every claim is source-tagged, and one primary-source figure held in memory will carry you through an opening speech and a first challenge. The rest deepens the position; those three make it defensible.
