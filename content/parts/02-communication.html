What is communication Comm p.2

It is a two-way street

Communication is expressing thoughts with words or actions. The definition contains a receiver, which is why sending alone is never enough.

Why "two-way" is the whole point in committee. A speech that ignores what the previous delegate said reads as a prepared statement, not a debate contribution. The Yield to Comments and Yield to Questions rules exist precisely to force the return leg of the street.

Verbal · oral Comm p.2 Added

Speaking so the room can follow you

Oral communication in committee is heard once, in real time, with no scrollback. Structure carries more weight than vocabulary.

Structure
  • Signpost firstSay what you will cover before you cover it. The chair and the room can then track you.
  • One idea per sentenceSubordinate clauses are for writing. Spoken argument fragments under them.
  • Land the claim before the evidenceClaim, then source, then implication. Reversing this loses listeners mid-way.
  • Close on the askEnd with the solution you want the committee to act on, not with a summary.
Delivery
  • PaceRoughly 130–150 words per minute is comfortable to follow. Ninety seconds is therefore about 200–220 words. Write to that count.
  • Pause instead of fillingA silent half-second reads as command. "Um" reads as searching.
  • Vary pitch on the claimMonotone flattens the sentence that mattered most.
  • Finish sentences down, not upRising terminals turn assertions into questions.
The arithmetic of ninety seconds
130–150Words per minute that a listener can comfortably follow
200–220Words that therefore fit in your 90 seconds
~2Substantive claims that fit alongside a POV and a Solution
15 secMinimum remaining time to Yield to Another Delegate
Verbal · written Comm p.2 Added

Written communication in committee

Written work is read by people who did not hear your speech and who can re-read you. Precision beats persuasion here.

What you will actually write
  • ChitsThe written channel during formal debate. Also the permitted way to answer a question under Yield to Questions.
  • Working PaperSolution to the sub-topic. Has no format, so clarity is entirely on you.
  • Draft ResolutionSolution to the main agenda. Has a particular format, and is voted on.
  • AmendmentsAdding, removing or changing a clause. Written so the change is unambiguous.
Discipline
  • One clause, one obligationA clause that does two things invites two objections and passes neither.
  • Name the actor"Urges member states to…" beats "It is urged that…". Passive drafting hides who must act.
  • Quantify where you canA timeframe or threshold makes the clause testable. Unfalsifiable clauses get amended away.
  • Spell-check before submissionA Friendly Amendment costs the committee time on a mistake you could have caught.
Non-verbal communication Comm p.2 Added

Nine channels you are broadcasting on anyway

The source lists nine non-verbal channels. Each is running whether you manage it or not, and each has a specific committee reading.

ChannelWhat the room reads from it
Personal appearanceWhether you treated the committee as a formal proceeding
ClothingAttire is Western Formals or School Uniform. Deviation is noticed before you speak.
HandshakeThe lobbying signal during unmoderated caucus. Firm, brief, eye contact.
Body languageOpenness or defensiveness while another delegate is challenging you
KinesicsMeaning carried by movement itself: nodding, leaning in, turning away
Eye contactAddress the chair and the committee, not your notes. Reading down is the commonest tell.
Face & voiceWhether your delivery matches your claim. Mismatch reads as insincerity.
Posture & gestureStillness reads as control; fidgeting reads as unpreparedness
Listening skillsVisible attention during others' speeches is itself a non-verbal statement
Trainer practice On a virtual committee, most of these collapse into a webcam frame. Fix the frame once: camera at eye level, face lit from the front, upper chest and shoulders visible so gesture reads.
Listening skills Comm p.2–3 Added

Listening is the half most delegates skip

The source names listening twice: once as a non-verbal channel, once among the skills you must possess, and again among the most valued soft skills. It is the most repeated item in the document.

Levels of listening
  1. IgnoringDrafting your own speech while another delegate talks
  2. Pretend listeningNodding without processing. Exposed the moment you are asked to respond.
  3. Selective listeningHearing only the part you already planned to rebut
  4. Attentive listeningFollowing the argument as given, including the parts you dislike
  5. Empathic listeningUnderstanding why that portfolio holds that position. This is what makes lobbying work.
What to capture while listening
  • Their claim: in one line, in their words
  • Their evidence, and whether they named a source
  • Their ask: what they want the committee to do
  • The gap: what they did not address
  • The overlap: where your solution and theirs could merge into one Working Paper
Point of Information requires you to prove a factual inaccuracy by stating your source. You cannot raise it if you were not listening closely enough to catch the inaccuracy.
Important skills one should possess Comm p.3 Added

Four skills, each with a committee test

  1. Listening skillsTest: after a Moderated Caucus, can you state each speaker's position without your notes?
  2. Writing skillsTest: can a delegate who missed your speech read your clause and know exactly what to do?
  3. Presentation skillsTest: can you hold ninety seconds without reading, and land POV plus Solution inside it?
  4. Personal skillsTest: can you disagree with a delegate's position in formal debate and still lobby with them in unmoderated caucus?
Trainer practice Run each test as a two-minute drill. The four together are the fastest diagnostic of where a delegate will actually lose points.
Which one to fix first In a committee setting these fail in a predictable order. Listening fails first, because it is invisible and feels optional. Presentation fails next, under time pressure. Writing fails at the drafting stage, when a clause has to survive being read by a stranger. Personal skills fail last, and only under challenge. Train them in that order.
Ladder of Inference Comm p.3 Chris Argyris

How observation becomes belief without you noticing

The Ladder of Inference, developed by Chris Argyris and later popularised by Peter Senge, describes the rungs we climb from raw data to action. We climb fast and rarely check.

  1. Observable dataEverything actually said or done
  2. Selected dataThe fraction you paid attention to
  3. Added meaningThe reading you place on it
  4. AssumptionsWhat you take as given based on that meaning
  5. ConclusionsWhat you decide is true
  6. BeliefsWhat you now hold about the world
  7. ActionsWhat you do, which shapes what you next observe
The reflexive loop. Your beliefs decide which data you select next time, so the ladder feeds itself. In committee this is how a delegate becomes certain a bloc is hostile on the strength of one unanswered chit.
Ladder of Inference · applied Added

Climbing back down the ladder

The value of the model is not knowing the rungs. It is having a question for each one when a debate stalls.

RungQuestion to ask yourself, or the room
ActionsWhat am I about to do, and what is it based on?
BeliefsWould I have held this before this committee started?
ConclusionsWhat would have to be true for the opposite conclusion to hold?
AssumptionsAm I treating my portfolio's interest as a fact about the world?
Added meaningIs this the only reading of what they said, or the most convenient one?
Selected dataWhat did they say that I skipped past?
Observable dataWhat are their exact words? Can I quote them back?
Quoting a delegate back to them accurately is the single most effective move in a Challenge. It forces the debate down to observable data, where sources decide the outcome.
Most valued soft skills in the 21st century Comm p.3

Four, and why each is hard

Confidence
  • Comes from preparation, not from temperament
  • Visible as stillness, eye contact, and finishing sentences
  • Its failure mode is bluffing a source you cannot name
Positive attitude
  • Staying constructive when your clause is amended out
  • Treating a hostile question as an opening, not an attack
  • Its failure mode is conceding the room to whoever is loudest
Good listening skills
  • The prerequisite for lobbying and for merging papers
  • The only way to catch a factual inaccuracy live
  • Its failure mode is rehearsing while others speak
Communication skills
  • Everything above, delivered inside ninety seconds
  • Verbal and non-verbal channels agreeing with each other
  • Its failure mode is a well-researched point nobody could follow
Applied · the Speakers List speech ROP p.5 Added

Ninety seconds, two mandatory aspects

The rule is fixed: your Speakers List speech gets 90 seconds and must carry your Portfolio's Point of View on the Agenda and your Solution on the Agenda. Here is a structure that fits both.

SecondsBeatContent
0–10Address"Delegate of [portfolio]". Establish who is speaking and to whom
10–30POVYour portfolio's position on the agenda, stated as a claim, not a summary of the agenda
30–55GroundingWhy that position follows from your portfolio's interests and record. Name one source.
55–80SolutionWhat you propose the committee do. Specific enough to become a clause.
80–90CloseRestate the ask in one line, then yield
If time remains, you must yield. Yield to Another Delegate needs 15 seconds or more. Yield to Questions gives 2 delegates a question each with 2 follow-ups and answering is mandatory. Yield to Comments gives 2 delegates 30 seconds each and you need not reply. Yield to the Chair when your time has elapsed.
Write it, time it, then cut it Draft to 200–220 words, read it aloud against a clock, then cut the sentence you like most that is doing the least work. Nearly every over-length first draft loses its Solution to the clock, which is the one half of the requirement the rules will not let you drop.

Key points for presentation Comm p.5

Eight questions your presentation must answer

  1. What is the key message of your SDG?
  2. Who is your target audience?
  3. What are the challenges in communicating your SDG?
  4. How can good communication help the world achieve your SDG?
  5. What tools or ways (posters, videos, speeches) can you use to share your SDG message?
  6. How can you make your SDG message simple and clear for everyone to understand?
  7. Why is it important to listen to others when talking about SDGs?
  8. Can you share an example of a real-life campaign that helped spread awareness about an SDG?
Trainer practice Question 8 asks for a real campaign. Hold teams to a named, checkable campaign with an organisation behind it. An invented example fails the same standard as an invented policy in committee.
